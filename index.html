Chapter One — The Forgotten Map

Rain drifted across the harbor city of Harrowport in long silver veils, softening the outlines of the ships anchored beyond the docks and turning the slate rooftops into dull mirrors that reflected the pale morning sky.

Above it all, on the highest ground the city possessed, the Royal Archive overlooked the waterfront like a second, sterner cathedral. Its granite walls had been weathered by centuries of salt wind and winter storms until the stone had gone the color of old pewter, and its narrow windows, set deep to keep out the damp, watched the harbor with the flat patience of something that had outlived every ship it had ever seen come in.

Adrian Hale had spent nearly a decade inside those halls, yet he had never grown accustomed to the feeling that the building itself was listening. He was not, by temperament, a man who chased marvels. A discovery that could not survive a second look was not a discovery. It was a wish wearing a discovery's coat, and Adrian had watched wishes ruin better scholars than himself.

Rather than examining the coastlines or the decorative illustrations, he directed the beam of a brass lamp across the parchment at a shallow angle. Experience had taught him that ink could be altered, scraped away, or replaced, but that the fibers beneath retained subtle scars no restorer, however skilled, could completely erase. Paper remembered.

Then, just as he was about to move the chart again, a faint blue curve shimmered beneath the faded paint before vanishing back into the texture of the parchment.

Adrian went very still.

The Story

A kingdom's most trusted maps were never entirely honest.

Harrowport, 1845. Adrian Hale has spent a decade restoring the kingdom's oldest charts inside the granite walls of the Royal Archive — patient work, unglamorous work, the kind that teaches a man to trust a contradiction over a comfortable answer. When his lamp catches a single erased curve on a map that scholars have studied for two hundred years, he assumes it's an old surveyor's mistake.

It is not a mistake. It is a crime three centuries in the making — a green country scraped from every chart in the realm by an office that has made an art of unmaking places, and the people who remember them, one careful correction at a time.

Pursued through Harrowport's rain-slick streets, guided by an old keeper's forbidden notebook, Adrian talks his way aboard the Terror and sails north with Captain Sela Torin, toward a towering wall of ice that no chart still in circulation dares to mark.

What waits behind it was never lost. It was hidden — and the difference between the two is the entire, dangerous heart of this book.

From the Author's Note
"This story began, for me, with a single question: what would it take to remove a place from the world? Not to destroy it, but to unmake it from memory… The answer, I came to believe, was not fire or war. It was something quieter and far more durable. It was bookkeeping."

— Joseph Cassius, author of Behind the Ice Wall

Main Characters

A restorer, a captain, and the archive that watches them both.

Adrian Hale

The Restorer

An archive-keeper's orphan raised inside the Royal Archive, Adrian has spent nearly ten years there as a restorer — exacting, unglamorous work that rewards patience over instinct. He's skeptical by nature, the kind of scholar who needs to see a thing twice before he'll believe it once. That same caution is what stops him cold when a mark on an old chart refuses to explain itself away.

Sela Torin

The Captain

Master of the Terror, and one of the few captains left willing to sail past where the honest charts end. Sela has spent her career navigating the gap between what the official maps say and what the sea actually shows her. She agrees to carry a restorer and his impossible request north — not out of curiosity, but because she's long suspected the charts were lying to her too.

Julian Keeler

The Rival

A sub-keeper at the Royal Archive with soft hands, a good name, and a talent for being underestimated. He has spent years quietly positioning himself to inherit Adrian's post, and doesn't appreciate the competition. A discovery this large, credited to someone else, is exactly the kind of complication Julian has built his career around avoiding.

Mercer

The Old Keeper

Thirty years a keeper himself, now living quietly above a chandler's shop with a debt he means to finally pay. Mercer is the one who hands Adrian the notebook that shouldn't exist — pages of careful, forbidden notes kept by someone who watched the erasures begin and couldn't stop them, only record them. What he knows could bring the whole quiet business of forgetting crashing down. What it costs him to share it is the reason he waited thirty years.
